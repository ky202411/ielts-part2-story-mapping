# Story-mapping patterns and language handling

Use these patterns only after reading the full cue-card prompts and recording the learner's actual facts. A **hub** is a familiar experience with several possible answer subjects. An answer should spend most of its time on the *subject the card requests*, not merely mention it at the start. A question that needs an unprovided fact stays **needs details**; an incompatible question stays **uncovered**.

## Language handling

- **Chinese input:** Extract the facts, relationships, timeline, and feelings in Chinese, then produce clear spoken English at the learner's level. Translate meaning, not Chinese sentence order. Ask for missing facts that determine topic fit.
- **English input:** First repair grammar, word choice, and unnatural phrasing while retaining the learner's intended meaning and personal voice. Then reduce unnecessary complexity and make the answer easy to pronounce, follow, and remember. Prefer familiar accurate words over ornate vocabulary; do not erase useful precise words. Do not turn a digital game action into a real-world action or otherwise change the facts.
- **Feedback format:** Give the final natural sentence or answer first. If useful for learning, show 2–4 representative original → improved edits with a short reason. Avoid flooding the learner with corrections. Do not label a stylistic preference as a grammar error.

Example based on the learner's game story:

| Learner's wording | Easier spoken version | Why |
|---|---|---|
| “Last week, a brand-new hide-and-seek game became incredibly popular online.” | “Last week, I tried a new hide-and-seek game on Steam.” | Starts with the learner's experience and avoids an unverified popularity claim. |
| “I am able to draw on my artistic skills and creativity to camouflage myself against the environment.” | “Because I study fine art, I could use my painting skills to blend my character into the background.” | Clearer spoken English; keeps the action inside the game. |
| “Devoting time to this game and tapping into my creativity fills my heart with tranquility and relaxation.” | “I found it relaxing because I could be creative.” | Easier to say naturally. |

These edits are suggestions, not a replacement for checking whether the full answer meets a particular card.

## Reusable mapping patterns

### 1. Place hub: one area, several distinct locations and moments

Build a place card with **where it is, what it looks or feels like, what the learner did there, who joined, and one memorable incident**. A city such as Guangzhou can be the setting for multiple cards, but a card asking for a *building* needs a specific building, and one asking for a *natural place* needs an actual natural site.

**Illustration only — hypothetical details to confirm:** The learner visits a Guangzhou riverside park with family, sees a landmark building, and gets caught in an unexpected rainstorm.

| Cue-card focus from the 53-card bank | Distinct answer subject | Additional fact required |
|---|---|---|
| Natural place in your city | The riverside park | It must actually be in the learner's city; visit frequency and companions. |
| Public building you enjoy visiting | A specific library or museum there | How they discovered it, its appearance, and why they enjoy visiting. The city itself is not a public building. |
| Place travelled to that you would recommend | Guangzhou or a specific site | What they saw and did, and a concrete reason to recommend it. |
| Crowded or noisy place | A particular market, station, or event | Actual time, companions, activities, and evidence of crowd or noise. |

The family outing may also support another event card **only if** its requested event happened and is recorded. “An interesting experience” or “a memorable trip with family” is a reusable *angle*, not automatic coverage of this bank's specific prompts.

## Place–event cluster

Use a memorable location as a **shared retrieval cue** for both place questions and event questions that could take place there. During the first pass, group every plausible event card under that location, even if the title does not explicitly say “place”. For each candidate, fill in **event verb + time + people + turning point/action + result**. This turns “Guangzhou Tower” into a small family outing cluster rather than one fixed place description.

Keep two labels separate: **in cluster** means worth checking; **covered** means all required event facts are confirmed. The same place may host a trip, an early start, a changed plan, a group activity, a sports event, or local news, but none follows automatically from having visited the place. When the card asks about an event, the event carries the answer and the place is one or two sentences of context. When the card asks about a place, describe its features and use the event as one concrete visit scene.

| Candidate card in the September–December bank | What a Guangzhou Tower cluster would need |
|---|---|
| **29. Place travelled to that you would recommend**; **36. Tall building you like/dislike** | The user's known visit, appearance, and badminton scene can support these place answers. |
| **10. Crowded place**; **23. Noisy place** | Confirm the conditions **during that visit** and the learner's reaction; annual visitor numbers do not establish either. |
| **38. Time you got up early** | Confirm an early start for this trip, its reason, what happened, and how the learner felt. |
| **45. Plan you had to change recently** | Confirm an original plan, a real replacement plan, recency, and reaction. |
| **46. Time you worked in a group**; **49. Live sport match** | Playing casual badminton with a brother is not necessarily group work or a watched match; these need distinct confirmed events. |
| **51. Piece of local news** | Confirm a real published/broadcast report, where it was seen or heard, people involved, and why it interested others. A personal visit is not news. |

For the learner's currently supplied Guangzhou Tower facts, put these cards in the **same candidate cluster** for memory, but keep unsupported event cards marked *needs details* or *uncovered* under the normal coverage rules. Recompute after the learner adds facts. A high-value follow-up asks about what happened at the location, rather than requesting an unrelated full story.

### 2. Person hub: one person, different observable qualities

Build a person card with **relationship, when/how known, repeated behavior, one concrete scene, and the learner's response**. One grandfather, friend, teacher, or parent may fit several person cards if different qualities are genuinely supported.

**Example using the learner's known facts:** Their grandfather was happy when the family arrived on his birthday. This can begin a “happy person” or “older person you admire” answer, but it does not yet prove he is generally happy or explain why he is admired. Ask for a habitual sign of happiness, a shared activity, or a specific admirable act. Do not call him a business owner, gardener, or history lover without evidence.

### 3. Event chain: reuse one sequence, shift to the requested moment

Record **plan → obstacle → response → outcome → reflection**. The same sequence may contain a journey, an occasion, a changed plan, and a decision. Each cue card requires a different centre of gravity.

**Example using the learner's Labor Day story:** The family planned to visit the grandfather for his birthday, hit traffic, waited for hours, eventually arrived, and he was happy. “Plan you had to change recently” is viable only if the learner confirms a *new plan*; arriving late alone may not be a changed plan. “Party for a special event” needs party details, including location and attendance. A card about a short trip taken *often* cannot use this one-off journey as-is. Keep all three distinctions explicit.

### 4. Object or media hub: one item, separate encounters with it

Record **what the item is, where it came from, one use or scene, why it matters, and any related purchase or media encounter**. A game, camera, book, movie, or video can link object, activity, and media prompts, but the requested media form must be real.

**Example using the learner's Steam game story:** The painting-based hide-and-seek game can support a creative activity or an interesting video **only if** the learner actually watched a video about it. It cannot answer “least favourite movie” merely because it has an on-screen story, or “saved money to buy something special” without a real saving-and-purchase account. If a friend took photos during play, that could support a photo-loving person only after the friend and habit are confirmed.

### 5. Decision-to-change hub: one turning point, several outcomes

Record **initial situation/opinion, options, why the choice was hard, actual decision, action, result, and feeling**. This can map to an important decision, a decision with a happy result, a changed opinion, a recent change, a solved problem, or a long-term goal. Check the card's tense and result: a future ambition is not an accomplished change; a happy result must actually be happy.

**Illustration only — hypothetical details to confirm:** A learner saved for a camera, chose a less expensive model after advice, used it on a trip, and liked the photos. If true, different cards could focus on saving, deciding, changing an opinion, or the outcome. Without the saving history or the earlier opinion, those cards remain **needs details**.

## Efficient selection

When choosing 3–4 stories, prefer hubs that contain **multiple real anchors**: a person, a place, an action or obstacle, an object, and a decision/result. Count only cue cards whose exact subject and prompts can be answered. A broadly reusable setting is valuable because it supports many *possible* angles, but it does not make every angle true.
