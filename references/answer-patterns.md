# Reusable answer patterns for IELTS Speaking Part 2

Use this reference to make **same-type answers structurally similar and easy to recall**. The type is determined by the cue card's requested subject, not by the source story's original label. Each pattern is a four-beat memory route inside the optional IMF timing: a brief introduction, two or three developed main beats, and a card-specific explanation or close. The English stems are interchangeable spoken supports, not a script to recite unchanged. Insert the actual “You should say” points wherever they naturally fit; if a point does not fit, ask for a fact instead of filling it with a generic sentence.

For a two-minute rehearsal, a useful starting allocation is **about 15 seconds to identify the subject, 90 seconds for specific details and one scene, and 15 seconds for the requested explanation**. These are adjustable practice targets, not quotas; let the recorded speaking pace determine the final length. Prefer short, accurate clauses and familiar words; avoid repeated ornamental openings, long lists of adjectives, or a conclusion that answers a different question.

| ID / card type | Four-beat route to remember | Reusable spoken supports | Cue-card example from the 53-card bank |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P — Person** | Who/how known → defining quality → one observed action/scene → why this person fits and how you feel | “The person I'd like to talk about is [name], my [relationship].” “What stands out about [him/her] is [quality].” “I noticed this when [specific scene].” “That's why I [admire/like/remember] [him/her].” | *A happy person you know*: the scene must show how the person expresses happiness; a single smile may be insufficient for a habitual trait. |
| **L — Place** | Exact place/where → visible or sensory features → a visit and activity → why you enjoy/recommend/dislike it | “The place I have in mind is [site] in [city].” “The first thing you notice is [concrete feature].” “When I went there, [action].” “I would [visit/recommend/avoid] it because [specific reason].” | *A public building you enjoy visiting*: name a particular building, say how you found it, describe it, and explain your visits. |
| **E — Event or experience** | When/where/who → initial plan/situation → turning point and response → outcome and exact feeling/lesson | “This happened [time] when I was [place] with [people].” “At first, [starting point].” “Then [change], so we [action].” “In the end, [result]; I felt [reason].” | *A plan you had to change recently*: a delay alone is not enough; the new plan must be stated. |
| **O — Object, book, film, or video** | Identify item/medium → what it is about or does → one use/viewing/reading scene → precise reaction or value | “The [item/book/video] I'd like to talk about is [name].” “It's about/used for [plain description].” “I first [used/read/watched] it when [scene].” “What made it [useful/interesting/disappointing] was [detail].” | *An interesting video*: describe the actual video and when/where it was watched, not merely a related game or film. |
| **D — Decision, change, or goal** | Earlier state → reason/trigger and alternatives → choice or plan and action → result or expected path, with significance | “I used to [earlier view/habit].” “After [trigger], I decided to [choice].” “To make it happen, I [action].” “The result was [actual result], which mattered because [reason].” | *An important decision with a happy result*: explain difficulty and an achieved result. For a long-term ambition, use a future plan instead of claiming it has happened. |
| **R — Proposed rule or law** | Problem → proposed rule → how it changes behavior/implementation → likely response and own view | “The problem I want to address is [issue].” “I would introduce a law that [specific rule].” “It would work by [mechanism].” “I think people would [reaction] because [reason].” | *An environmental law you would like your country to introduce*: include why people should follow it and whether it would be popular. |

Keep **one stable route per type**, but allow wording to vary a little so it sounds spoken rather than chanted. The cue-card prompts override the table: for example, a person card asking *when you normally see them* needs that fact in the body; a place card asking *how you discovered it* needs that story. Same-type answers should feel like variations of a familiar path, not copies of one essay.

## Cross-type pivots: same factual world, new answer centre

For any pivot, run this check: **Does the original fact ledger contain the destination subject and every binding prompt?** If yes, use the destination pattern and devote most of the answer to its subject. The source story becomes one or two sentences of context. If no, mark the card *needs details* or *uncovered*.

Treat all event cards that can naturally mention the same place as one **practice cluster**: repeat the location cue in the memory notes, then use **E** for each distinct event. This creates a familiar sentence rhythm — “This happened at [place] when ... / At first ... / Then ... / In the end ...” — without confusing event types. For a place card in that cluster, switch back to **L** and make the setting, appearance, and reason to visit the centre of the answer.

| Pivot | What changes in the answer | Fit condition |
|---|---|---|
| **Place → event/trip** | Introduce the location briefly, then use **E** to narrate what happened there and its outcome. | There was an actual noteworthy event or journey, with time, people, action, and result. A scenic description by itself is not an event. |
| **Place → local news** | Use **E** to explain the reported development, where the learner heard it, who was involved, and why others cared; the place is context. | There was a real piece of local news. A personal outing or an imagined headline does not satisfy the news card. |
| **Event → person** | Use **P**: identify the person and their quality, then retell only the part of the event that demonstrates it. | The person's requested trait or role is genuinely evidenced, not inferred from one vague reaction. |
| **Person → event** | Use **E**: name the shared occasion, sequence, and outcome; describe the person as a participant. | The requested occasion or difficulty actually occurred. |
| **Object/media → problem or decision** | Use **E** for a breakdown or **D** for a purchase/choice. Mention the object briefly, then foreground the action and result. | There was a real malfunction, purchase, or decision. The mere existence of the item is not enough. |
| **Decision → change or goal** | Keep **D**, but choose the right tense and endpoint: a completed change has an observed outcome; a goal has a future plan. | The timeline and outcome match the exact card. |

### Worked illustration: Guangzhou as a shared setting

**Hypothetical details for demonstration only:** A learner visited a riverside park in Guangzhou with family; rain began; they changed their afternoon plan. If these facts are confirmed, a **place** answer could follow **L**: where the park is → river and paths → family visit → why they recommend it. A **changed-plan** answer would follow **E**: original afternoon plan → sudden rain → replacement activity → reaction. For **local news**, neither answer is enough: the learner would need a real report about that area, its source, people involved, and public interest. This example shows why a single setting can cross types while the answer structure must switch.

The two answers can reuse a few familiar words while changing their centre: **L** — “The place I'd like to recommend is a riverside park in Guangzhou. What stands out is the view across the water. I went there with my family, and I liked how calm it felt.” **E** — “This happened during a family trip to Guangzhou. At first, we planned to have a picnic by the river. Then it began to rain, so we changed our plan and went somewhere indoors.” These are short pattern demonstrations, not complete two-minute answers or claims about the learner's life. A local-news answer would require a separate, genuine news report and could not be generated from this outing alone.

## Memorisation format

Give learners a short cue such as **L / riverside park / Guangzhou → river view → family walk → peaceful** or **E / rain → original plan → replacement → result**. Rehearse from the cue, not from a memorised paragraph. After a timed recording, shorten phrases that cause hesitation and add a concrete detail where the talk is thin. Preserve the same four-beat route across questions of the same type.
