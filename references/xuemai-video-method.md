## What the visible chapter structure shows

The creator groups related Part 2 questions into 11 practice clusters, then treats several difficult prompts separately. The reusable unit is a **shared subject or event plus flexible keywords**; each cue card still needs its own opening, selected details, and final explanation. The visible summary mentions flexible use of material and keywords. In the skill, use this as a two-pass method:

1. **Cluster first:** Group cards that could naturally use one real anchor (same person, place, event, object, decision, or issue). The chapter labels show cross-category clusters as well as single-category ones.
2. **Verify each fit:** For each card, test the exact subject, time/relationship constraints, and all prompts against the fact ledger. Reuse only confirmed or authorized details. Change the answer's focus, not the story's facts.
3. **Leave difficult cards separate:** If a card requires a distinct person, occupation, object, or event absent from the core stories, prepare a short additional story instead of forcing a weak link. This is an inference from the video's separate “难题” chapters, not a claim that the creator prescribes a particular number of stories.

## Core extraction from user-supplied notes

The learner later supplied a detailed account of the video's examples, including an iPad incident, exam burnout, a public figure and advertisement, two places, a family dinner rule, and several smaller stories. Treat those examples as **user-supplied notes, not independently verified video transcription**. Do not copy their long sample answers into generated output or treat the sample events as the learner's own experiences. Their transferable method is:

1. **Build a mother-story fact card, not a mother-script.** Store a compact chain: subject and setting → motive or initial state → specific event/obstacle → action → outcome → feeling/meaning. Tag every real person, place, object, and decision it contains. One richly anchored incident is more useful than a generic paragraph.
2. **Cluster by an actual shared anchor.** The user-provided iPad example combines purchase, unexpected cost, a Wi-Fi failure, and the mother's troubleshooting. Those are distinct events around one object, so different questions can select different parts. The object alone does not make all technology or family questions fit.
3. **Recast the centre of the answer.** For a technology-problem card, briefly identify the iPad, spend most of the talk on failure and repair, and close with the resolution. For a person-who-helped card, the mother becomes the subject; emphasize what she did and why her help mattered. For an overspending card, focus on the buying decision and cost, with the Wi-Fi episode likely omitted. Changing only the first and last sentence would leave the wrong centre of gravity.
4. **Use a card-specific bridge.** The opening should directly name the requested subject and explain the connection to the shared story. Then choose details that answer the visible prompts in their natural order; the final explanation answers the card's actual *why/how/feeling* question. A bridge cannot create a missing event.
5. **Separate facts from variants.** The notes sometimes switch from self to friend or add a new video, film viewing, app, or law. These are valid mapping ideas only if the learner confirms they happened or authorizes a fictional practice variant. Keep facts stable across all answers. A 2026 May–August example prompt is not automatically in the September–December 53-card bank.
6. **Use small independent stories for hard gaps.** If a card needs a medical-career person, a specific party, or a film the learner disliked and the core stories do not contain one, request the missing real details or prepare a separate small story. Three or four stories are a target for memory efficiency, not a reason to distort a prompt.
