# IELTS Speaking Part 2 story mapping

Help the learner prepare a **specified Part 2 cue-card bank** with three or four memorable personal stories where they fit. Optimize for natural relevance and low memorization burden; retain small standalone stories for genuine gaps. “All questions” means every unique cue card in the bank used for this run; never imply that an unofficial list is the complete live exam pool.

## Establish the bank and stories

- For **September–December 2026**, first read the [user-supplied Part 2 cue-card snapshot](references/2026-09-12-part2.md). It contains 53 unique full cards; use their supplied prompts rather than relying on title guesses. For a later season or an explicitly requested refresh, follow [the 同桌英语 source procedure](references/topic-sources.md). The learner need not search for or supply a bank again. Do not add topics from other sites.
- Accept rough Chinese or English accounts. Extract 3–4 core stories from the user's experiences. If only 1–2 are supplied, start mapping them, identify the largest topic gaps, and ask targeted questions that can develop complementary stories. Do not require polished narratives.
- If the learner writes in English, correct grammar and awkward phrasing while preserving the facts and intended meaning; then make the answer easier to say aloud with clear, natural wording. If the learner writes in Chinese, translate the same facts into natural spoken English without adding events. When helpful, show a few high-impact original → improved examples rather than a line-by-line correction of every sentence. See [language handling and examples](references/story-mapping-patterns.md#language-handling).
- Record a compact **fact ledger** for each story: people and relationships, time, place, object, sequence, actions, outcome, feelings, and exact user wording where ambiguity matters. Distinguish confirmed facts, reasonable wording, and missing details. Keep these facts consistent across all answers.
- Story types are useful *tags*, not fixed quotas: people/relationships, travel/place, challenge/growth, celebration/activity, object/media, interest/learning. One story can carry several tags. Prefer stories with several concrete anchors and distinct coverage.

## Map before writing

1. For each accessible topic, identify its primary subject (person, place, object, event, activity, skill, cultural work, or future plan), visible cue-card prompts, and binding constraints such as childhood, recent, first time, gift, or wish. When the site exposes only a title, mark its prompt detail **unknown** rather than inventing cue-card bullets.
2. Match candidate stories by **answer focus**, not by a shared keyword. A story fits only if its subject and constraints can be addressed without changing confirmed facts. Reframe the same event around a different real anchor when appropriate: a traffic delay can answer a delayed journey; the grandfather's reaction can anchor a family celebration after the celebration details are known.
   For reusable angles across person, place, event, object/media, and decision questions, use [story-mapping patterns](references/story-mapping-patterns.md). Treat each pattern as a way to inspect genuine facts, not as permission to invent them.
   Build a **place–event cluster** for each distinctive location: put its place cards and every event card that could naturally be set there in one candidate group. Record the actual action/turning point for each event card; the location is the shared memory cue, not proof that the event happened. Apply the [place–event cluster rules](references/story-mapping-patterns.md#placeevent-cluster).
   For an additional clustering example, consult [薛麦 Max's chapter-based Part 2 grouping](references/xuemai-video-method.md). It is a preparation method from a May–August 2026 video, not a source for the current cue-card bank or a verified transcript of her spoken examples.
   The learner later supplied detailed notes about the video's example materials. Use the [core extraction from those notes](references/xuemai-video-method.md#core-extraction-from-user-supplied-notes) to build a fact-based mother story and recast its main body for each card; do not rely on opening and closing changes alone.
3. Mark each question **direct**, **needs one or more details**, or **uncovered**. “Direct” requires enough confirmed, prompt-specific detail for a natural full answer, not merely a matching subject. State precisely what is missing, including a concrete scene or action if the account is too thin. Prefer 3–4 stories that cover the most distinct questions with the fewest new facts and least awkward reframing; report any remaining cards for standalone preparation. Do not count “needs details” as covered until those details are supplied or explicitly authorized for invention.
4. Ask only high-value follow-up questions, preferably one at a time: a detail is valuable when it makes several currently weak mappings natural. Recompute the mapping when new facts arrive. If a prompt still has no natural fit, show the gap; invite another small story or an authorized fictional variant rather than forcing it.

## Write the answer set

- Read [reusable answer patterns](references/answer-patterns.md) before drafting the set. Give each cue card a primary answer type (person, place, event/experience, object/media, decision/change/goal, or proposed law). Answers of the same type should share a familiar four-beat order and a small set of natural sentence frames, while their concrete evidence and final explanation answer the individual card. For a place–event cluster, keep one location cue across answers but use the **place** pattern for a place card and the **event** pattern for an event card; move the location into brief background when the event is the subject.
- For every covered cue card, write an English spoken answer that addresses the card's actual subject and prompts. Use a clear beginning, a specific scene or action, and a personal reason or reflection. Shift emphasis between cards; avoid reusing an unchanged essay with a new first sentence.
- Aim for a natural 1–2 minute response at the learner's stated speaking pace and level. If pace or target band is unknown, use clear everyday English and a practice length that can be adjusted after speaking aloud. Avoid inflated vocabulary and repetitive stock phrases.
- Preserve the fact ledger across the set. The user may authorize plausible invented details; label them in preparation materials and keep them stable thereafter. Without that authorization, present missing details as questions or optional suggestions, not as events that happened.
- Alongside full answers, give a compact memory cue for each card: story ID, answer-pattern ID, focus, 3–5 keywords, and the one detail that makes it specific to the prompt. Group cards by story to reduce memorization.
- Write spoken answers in English; give mapping notes and brief corrections in the learner's input language unless they request otherwise. Show the improved English first so it is easy to practise.
- For preparation notes, timed rehearsal, and answer-length coaching, apply the evidence-based guidance in [Part 2 speaking techniques](references/part2-techniques.md). Treat reusable stories as flexible material, not speeches to recite unchanged.
- When a learner wants a ready-to-practise structure, offer the [IMF outline](references/part2-techniques.md#optional-imf-outline-user-provided): a direct introduction, prompt-specific main points, and a feeling or future reflection. Adapt the ending to the card and keep the total within two minutes.

## Output and quality check

Start with a story index and a coverage table: topic title, title-only or full wording, chosen story, focus, status, and missing/added detail. Display place cards and location-linked event candidates together under their shared place–event cluster, while keeping each card's status separate. Report counts against the stated bank denominator: direct, completed after details, and uncovered. Then provide all requested answers, grouped by story, plus memory cues. For a large bank, deliver numbered batches and maintain the same ledger and denominator across batches.

Before delivery, check each answer against its cue-card constraints, the original user facts, internal timeline and relationships, natural spoken English, and plausible speaking time. Clearly distinguish sourced current-season topics from example prompts. Do not promise a band score or claim complete seasonal coverage without a verified denominator.

### Brief mapping example

Story facts: On Labor Day, the learner travelled with their parents to their hometown for their grandfather's birthday; traffic delayed them for hours; the grandfather was happy when they arrived.

| Cue card from the September–December bank | Status | Focus / needed fact |
|---|---|---|
| Party for a special event that impressed you | Needs details | What the party involved, where it was, how many attended, and why it impressed the learner. |
| A plan that you had to change recently | Needs details | What the replacement plan was and whether Labor Day was recent enough at the learner's test date. |
| A happy person you know | Needs details | The arrival shows one happy moment; ask how the grandfather usually shows happiness. |
| A short trip you often take but do not enjoy | Uncovered | A single Labor Day journey does not satisfy “often”. |

The party answer must focus on the celebration once its details are known; the changed-plan answer needs a genuine new plan. Neither can be created by changing only the opening sentence.
